## SECTION 1 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The family is **partially fit for purpose: adequate as internal operational hygiene for generative AI, and not fit for purpose as a framework governing automated systems that make or inform determinations about Georgians.** That split is structural rather than a drafting matter. Across seven instruments, every duty an agency owes itself — vet the tool, review the output, protect the data, train the staff, report the misuse — sits in a binding tier with an identifiable actor. Every duty the state owes a resident — tell them an automated system is involved, explain an adverse outcome, give them a route to contest it, give them a human instead — sits only in guidance, only on a public website, or nowhere. A resident harmed by an automated eligibility determination could read all seven instruments and find nothing they are entitled to invoke.

The 2025 instruments improved the family materially and show that GTA's maintenance capability is real: SS-25-001 added a credential-based shadow-AI control, a 48-hour incident clock, a red-teaming sandbox, and a records-classification duty, and GS-25-002 added workforce change management and an impact assessment to procurement. Several are ahead of peer-state practice. But all three are scoped to generative AI or to procurement, so the enterprise has spent two years deepening its controls over the technology that writes drafts while leaving untouched the technology that scores applicants. The instruments now govern what a model outputs. They do not govern what a system decides, and they do not govern what a system does.

Ordered by priority, the seven most consequential findings are these. **F-01** (D5, E1, P1): no instrument provides contestability, appeal, human re-determination, or a human alternative for an affected resident — a constitutional due-process exposure wherever automated systems inform the reduction or termination of benefits, licences, or employment. **F-02** (D6, E1, P1): PS-23-001, the procurement gate, defines "Artificial Intelligence" using the words the other instruments assign to *generative* AI, so a predictive eligibility or fraud model is not within the pre-procurement review requirement on the face of the controlling policy. **F-03** (D1, E1, P1): agentic operation is absent from the binding tier, and the corpus's only statement on it — that AI should never have autonomous decision-making capability — sits in a non-binding rubric and is contradicted by measured deployment practice in software the state already licenses. **F-04** (D5, E1, P1): the ADA Title II technical standard becomes mandatory for Georgia state agencies on 26 April 2027, and no instrument addresses accessibility of AI interfaces or AI-generated content beyond one generic procurement clause. **F-05** (D9, E1, P1): notice and explanation duties owed to residents exist only in the guideline tier and on the public site, creating reliance exposure with no operational counterpart. **F-06** (D2, E1, P1): binding human review attaches to generative content but not to predictive or automated decision systems, so the systems most likely to determine eligibility carry the weakest oversight duty in the family. **F-07** (D4, E1, P1): SS-23-002 imposes detailed duties on non-agency parties with no contract clause, procurement condition, or statutory hook, and its NIST requirement names no version, profile, or acceptance criterion.

**The single most significant structural weakness** is the disappearance of the resident from the family, and it has a traceable cause. GTA's five guiding principles derive, one for one, from the OSTP *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* (October 2022). Four survived adaptation. The fifth — *Human Alternatives, Consideration, and Fallback*, the right to opt out of an automated process and reach a person who can consider and remedy the problem — was replaced by a principle of the same nominal subject, "Keep Human Involvement at the Center," every element of which is internal-facing. The right became a duty the agency owes itself, and nothing fills the vacated role. The source framework has since been withdrawn from current federal policy, so the family's normative architecture now rests on a document GTA cannot cite as authority.

**What is working and should be preserved.** SS-23-002 §3(b)(i)(4) requires third-party providers to run unit and integration tests, proactively detect input drift, maintain a gold-standard test set refreshed to reduce the risk of training on the test set, and build failure-triggering checks into the environment. Drafted in 2023, it anticipates evaluation-contamination and drift concerns that reached mainstream public-sector policy considerably later, and it is more specific than most peer-state equivalents; retain it verbatim and extend it rather than rewrite it. SS-23-002 §10 correctly pairs a named termination authority with a contingency obligation under an existing standard. SS-25-001 §1.4 is an auditable shadow-AI control, §2.3's sandbox red-teaming is a capability rather than a paper control, and GS-25-002 §7 makes workforce change management a scored procurement criterion. The "Agency" definition is consistent across four instruments, and GTA's documented PSG revision process exists and has simply not been pointed at this family.

**Decision points requiring State CIO or Advisory Council resolution.** (1) Does the state owe a resident affected by an automated determination a human alternative and a route to contest, and which instrument carries it? (2) Should PS-23-001's definition be widened, or should the procurement gate move into SS-23-002 where the broad definition already sits? (3) Is any state conversational system an "operator" under SB 540, and independent of that answer, will Georgia hold itself to the standard it imposes on private operators from 1 July 2027? (4) Does GTA intend to exercise the independent-evaluation right it has reserved since 2023, and with what headcount — or should the right become a duty with a trigger? (5) Are agentic capabilities permitted in the state estate, and under what authority ceiling? (6) Who owns the 26 April 2027 ADA Title II date?

### 1(f) Corrections to the anchor register

Four corrections arise from the Part 3.2 verification. They are recorded as AMEND entries at §7.2.

1. **SB 540 scope — material.** The register describes the introduced bill. The enacted act was narrowed by House substitute, agreed by the Senate on 27 March 2026: the operative term became "AI companion chatbot" rather than "conversational AI service," with express exclusions for internal business tools, customer service chatbots, and educational tools. The register's statement that there is no carve-out is accurate only as to platform size and general-purpose platforms; subject-matter exclusions do apply, and they materially reduce the likelihood that a routine agency support chatbot is directly covered. This correction lowers the direct-application finding and raises the comparative-posture finding.

Because population is calculated at state level, Georgia state agencies fall in the ≥50,000 tier and the operative date is **26 April 2027**. The register's date and tier are correct; the locator and the state-level population calculation should be added, and the rule's interim status flagged.
3. **Provenance framework — resolved.** The framework is the OSTP *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* (October 2022), five principles, one-for-one structural match. Its principles were carried into the October 2023 federal AI executive order, which was rescinded in January 2025; the Blueprint no longer sits on the current White House site. Status: historical document, not current federal authority. W-08 closes.
4. **NIST COSAiS — precision.** Concept paper 14 August 2025; an annotated outline discussion draft for the *predictive AI* use case published 8 January 2026 with comment closing 13 February 2026. The **single-agent and multi-agent overlays remain in development with no announced publication date** as of the most recent confirmed reporting. The register's status line should distinguish the predictive-AI outline (exists) from the agent overlays (do not).

### C5 — Public-facing consistency

Three restatements of the same five principles now exist: GS-23-001 (2023), ai.georgia.gov, and GS-25-001 (2025). They differ substantively.

- **Force downgraded.** GS-23-001 Principle V.1 and V.2 use "shall"; the public page renders the same content as "should." A resident reading the state's public commitment sees a weaker undertaking than the internal guideline states — and the internal guideline is itself unenforceable.
- **Structure corrupted in the newest instrument.** GS-25-001 presents the principles with "Ensure Ethical and Fair Use of Automated Decisions" nested as a sub-bullet beneath "Implement Responsible Systems," yielding **four top-level principles** in a document that asserts there are five. **F-21.**

None of those is delivered by a binding instrument, and none is invocable by the resident to whom the promise is made. That combination — a public commitment with no operational counterpart — is D9 as to allocation and **D8 as to exposure**: in an Open Records request, a legislative hearing, or litigation, the state's own published principles become the standard it is measured against. **E1** (both texts confirmed live).

### C6 — Framework provenance

**Identification.** The five principles derive from the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* (October 2022). The mapping is one for one:

| Blueprint principle | GTA principle |
|---|---|
| Safe and Effective Systems | I. Implement Responsible Systems |
| Algorithmic Discrimination Protections | II. Ensure Ethical and Fair Use of Automated Decisions |
| Data Privacy | III. Maintain Data Quality and Privacy |
| Notice and Explanation | IV. Keep AI Usage Transparent |
| Human Alternatives, Consideration, and Fallback | V. Keep Human Involvement at the Center |

The terminology confirms it: "automated systems" as the operative object, "notice and explanation," "algorithmic discrimination," and the opportunities-and-access-to-essential-services scope trigger are all Blueprint constructions, not NIST or ISO ones.

**Status change.** The Blueprint was non-binding OSTP guidance. Its principles were incorporated into the federal AI executive order of October 2023, which was rescinded by executive order in January 2025; the Blueprint no longer resides on the current White House site and is available through archives. It is therefore a **historical document and not current federal authority.** This does not reduce GTA's obligations, because the substance of principles II, IV and V is independently secured by binding law that has not changed — constitutional due process, Title VI, Title VII, and the ADA. But it means GTA's guideline tier now rests on a withdrawn framework, and the most developed public-sector successor model, OMB M-25-21 and M-25-22, is structured differently, around a **high-impact AI classification with attaching minimum practices** rather than around five principles. Those memoranda bind federal agencies only and are cited here as persuasive.

**Dropped element, and whether it is material.** The right to a human alternative, consideration, and fallback **did not survive adaptation, and nothing fills its role.** The Blueprint's fifth principle was an entitlement running to the individual: the ability to opt out of an automated system where appropriate, access to a person who can consider and remedy problems, and a fallback that is accessible, timely, equitable, and not punitive. GTA's Principle V retains the word "human" and inverts the beneficiary. All ten of its sub-elements — human responsibility, records, oversight, ethical design, roles, human-AI collaboration, user education, training, ownership of data and models, continuous monitoring — describe what the agency owes itself. "User education" is the closest to resident-facing, and it educates users about the system rather than giving them recourse against it. No opt-out, no human alternative, no remedy route, and no named person a resident can reach appears anywhere in the seven instruments.

Two further Blueprint elements were weakened in adaptation. Proactive equity assessment and disparity testing with published results became "regularly review and evaluate AI-generated outputs" with no method. Independent evaluation and reporting became a right GTA reserves rather than a duty it owes. **This is the single most significant structural weakness of the family and the origin of F-01, F-05, and F-13.** **Evidence tier E2** — the provenance identification is documentary and inferential from structure and terminology rather than from a citation in the instruments themselves, which nowhere name their source.

### C7 — Currency and maintenance

- **Review dates.** One of seven instruments carries a review date. It has lapsed by 18 months and no amendment followed. Six carry none.
- **Versioning.** No instrument carries a version number. The GS-25-001 URL bears a "-0" suffix, which is consistent with an earlier node having been replaced; whether GS-25-001 supersedes an unversioned predecessor could not be established.
- **Off-cycle revision triggers.** None. Nothing in the corpus is revisited on enactment of relevant legislation, publication of a NIST overlay, a federal rulemaking, or an incident.
- **Ownership of revision.** Not stated in any instrument. GTA maintains published PSG processes for creation, revision, approval, and continuing-need review — the machinery exists and is a genuine strength — but no instrument in this family names who triggers it or when.
- **Pace test.** The 2025–2026 biennium enacted two statutes imposing AI-specific duties with 2027 commencement dates, SB 540 (1 July 2027) and SB 444 (1 January 2027), and adopted SR 789 establishing a study committee whose recommendations feed the session convening January 2027. **No instrument in the family references any of them.** The family added three instruments in July 2025 and none since. On the current record, the maintenance cadence is roughly biennial and reactive; the legislative cadence is annual and accelerating. **E1** as to the statutes; **F1** as to the 2027 session, on the basis of a published legislative calendar and an adopted study-committee mandate.

**(a)–(e) Notice, explanation, contestability, human alternative, due process.** *Why material to Georgia specifically:* the governed population administers benefits, licensing, employment and public safety, and O.C.G.A. § 50-13 already supplies a contested-case architecture into which an AI-specific re-determination route could be fitted — the procedural machinery exists and is simply not connected to automated systems. *Authority:* constitutional procedural due process where automated systems affect eligibility for, reduction of, or termination of benefits, licences or employment [E1]; O.C.G.A. § 50-13 [E1]; OSTP Blueprint Principle 5, historical and not current federal authority [E2]; Colorado SB 26-189 and California CPPA automated decision-making regulations, persuasive and comparative only [E2]. *Consequence of continued silence:* the state has no defensible record that a human considered an adverse automated determination, no route by which a resident can trigger one, and a published commitment to notice and explanation it does not operationalise. *Destination:* SS-23-002, new §13, with an implementing GTA procedure and an agency-level SOP referencing existing contested-case procedure.

**(f) Language access.** *Why material:* machine translation is the cheapest apparent solution to a Title VI obligation and the least reliable in exactly the communications that matter — notices of adverse action, eligibility determinations, appeal rights. *Authority:* Title VI of the Civil Rights Act [E1]. *Consequence:* a mistranslated notice of adverse action is a notice failure, not a translation failure, and the family provides no accuracy standard, no human-review requirement for consequential translations, and no prohibition on unreviewed machine translation of legal notices. *Destination:* SS-23-002 §5.

**(g) Accessibility.** *Why material:* population is calculated at state level under the Title II rule, so Georgia state agencies sit in the ≥50,000 tier and the operative date is 26 April 2027 — inside the next four quarterly runs. The rule reaches content delivered through third-party arrangements, which is how most agency AI reaches the public. *Authority:* ADA Title II, 28 C.F.R. § 35.200(b) as amended at 91 Fed. Reg. 20902 (20 Apr. 2026); interim final rule, comment period closed June 2026, final status to be confirmed; WCAG 2.1 AA incorporated by reference [E1]. *Consequence:* chatbots, AI-generated PDFs, machine captions and AI-mediated interfaces non-conformant at a date certain, with private Title II litigation unaffected by the extension. Note the internal irony: SS-25-001 requires accessibility compliance in procurement and publishes its own operative use-case guidance as a raster image. *Destination:* SS-23-002 §5 and SS-25-001 §1.5.

**(h) Minors.** *Why material:* Georgia has now legislated a standard for private operators of AI companion chatbots — disclosure, age assurance, engagement-reward prohibition, self-harm response protocol — effective 1 July 2027, while its own guidance permits agency support chatbots with none of those features. *Authority:* SB 540, adopted 11 May 2026, effective 1 July 2027, in Chapter 5 of Title 39, narrowed by House substitute to AI companion chatbots with exclusions for internal business tools, customer service chatbots and educational tools; Attorney-General enforcement [E2]. *Consequence:* whether or not a given state system is a covered "operator" — a question for counsel — the reputational and policy exposure of a state system without a crisis-referral protocol is not contingent on that answer. *Destination:* SS-23-002, new §15.

Requiring vendors to "provide copies of their framework" collects documents, not assurance. *Authority:* 2025 AI Agent Index [E1]; state purchasing law and DOAS rules [E1]; GovAI Coalition vendor disclosure and contract templates [E2]; ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as a certifiable procurement signal [E1]; Texas TRAIGA's substantial-compliance safe harbour as a model for giving framework alignment operational weight — persuasive only [E1]. *Consequence:* no basis to reject a deficient submission, no notice when a model version changes beneath a live process, and no visibility into subprocessors or upstream model dependencies. *Destination:* a standard contract clause set owned jointly by State Purchasing and the Office of AI, referenced from SS-23-002 §3.

Reported red-team success rates for novel agent attack strategies substantially exceeding baseline defence performance [E3 — the primary NIST figures require confirmation before citation in any external document]. *Consequence of continued silence:* GTA's only statement on autonomy is a non-binding sentence saying it should never occur, which provides no authority to permit a controlled deployment and no control over an uncontrolled one. **R-32.** GTA should monitor whether federal preemption reaches state government use of AI, which the current executive order expressly carves out. *Tier:* F2 — speculative. *Trigger that converts to F1:* a preemption statute enacted, or a court ruling in litigation arising from the executive order that addresses the scope of its carve-outs. *Monitoring signal:* DOJ AI Litigation Task Force filings; the Commerce evaluation of state AI laws; Congressional preemption vehicles. *Owner:* Agency counsel. *Destination:* no instrument unless the carve-out narrows; a counsel memorandum otherwise. *Dependency:* none. *Completion indicator:* a standing counsel note, refreshed each quarterly run, recording whether the carve-out for state government use still holds. **Note:** as of this run no federal preemption of state AI law has been enacted, and the order's stated carve-outs include state government procurement and use of AI — the exact activity these instruments govern. The preemption question therefore bears on Georgia's regulation of private actors, not on GTA's governance of its own enterprise.

**R-33.** GTA should anticipate that autonomous-action capability will arrive in the state estate by default rather than by decision, and should treat capability-activation notice from vendors as a contractual requirement rather than a courtesy. *Tier:* F2 — the direction is evidenced but the timing is not. *Trigger that converts to F1:* any product on a statewide contract enabling autonomous action or tool use by default in a general release. *Monitoring signal:* vendor release notes for the top ten products in the state estate; the annual AI Agent Index. *Owner:* GTA OIS; State Purchasing. *Destination:* the standard contract clause set at §6.6(f) and SS-23-002 §14. *Dependency:* vendor agreement to the notification term, obtainable only at renewal. *Completion indicator:* capability-activation notice is a term in every AI-bearing statewide contract, and at least one notice has been received and acted on.

### Sequencing note

Three items must come first because others depend on them. **R-1** must precede everything scoped by the word "AI," because until PS-23-001's definition is corrected, every downstream control inherits a gate that excludes predictive systems — including R-2, R-5, R-13, R-15 and R-16, all of which are intended to reach exactly those systems. **R-13** must precede R-4, R-15, R-16, R-20, R-21 and R-26, because each of those requires an artifact to be held against a system, and there is currently no register in which to hold it; the register is the substrate for almost every other assurance recommendation in this run. **R-9** must precede R-2, R-7, R-12 and R-19, because each assigns a duty to a role, and four of the roles the instruments currently name do not exist. R-3 is sequenced immediately but independently: it is a holding action that costs nothing and prevents the one class of exposure that cannot be remediated after the fact.

## SECTION 6 — PROPOSED REPLACEMENT LANGUAGE

Drafted to the standard of instruments that will be read adversarially in litigation, an Open Records request, or a legislative hearing. Each provision specifies the accountable role, the trigger, the threshold, the frequency, and the record produced.

### 6.1 — PS-23-001, Terms and Definitions, and Policy (F-02, P1)

**Current text.** Definitions: "Artificial Intelligence: tools or systems used to create models that can generate new and original content, such as images, music, or text, based on patterns and examples from existing data." Policy: agencies shall submit all AI software, applications, tools and services to GTA for review prior to procurement.

**Defect summary.** The controlling policy defines "Artificial Intelligence" using the words SS-23-002 and SS-25-001 both assign to *Generative AI*, so predictive and classificatory systems fall outside the procurement gate as written, and the gate is triggered only by a procurement event.

**Proposed text.**

> **Terms and Definitions**
>
> **Artificial Intelligence:** a machine-based system that, for a given set of human-defined objectives, makes predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments, or that generates content. This definition includes predictive, classificatory, generative, multimodal, and agentic systems, and applies whether the capability is acquired as a distinct product or activated within a product the state already licenses.
>
> **Generative AI:** a subset of Artificial Intelligence comprising tools or systems used to create models that generate new and original content from patterns and examples in existing data.
>
> **Policy**
>
> Agencies shall submit to GTA for review, prior to acquisition or activation, all software, applications, tools, and services that incorporate Artificial Intelligence as defined in this policy. The duty to submit is triggered by whichever occurs first: a procurement action, the renewal of an existing agreement that adds or enables an Artificial Intelligence capability, or the agency becoming aware that an Artificial Intelligence capability has been activated within a product it already licenses. An agency that becomes aware of an activated capability shall notify the GTA Office of Artificial Intelligence within ten business days and shall record the notification in the enterprise Artificial Intelligence register. All agencies shall follow established State policies and standards to implement technology resources using Artificial Intelligence.

**Rationale.** Aligning the policy tier's definition with the standard tier's closes the gap through which the systems of greatest consequence to residents currently pass unreviewed. Adding activation as an independent trigger addresses the acquisition pathway that now dominates: capability arriving inside licensed software without a contract action. Ten business days is short enough to be a real control and long enough to be achievable by an agency without a dedicated function.

**Authority mapped.** SS-23-002 Terms and Definitions [E1]; SS-25-001 Terms and Definitions and §1.5 [E1]; O.C.G.A. § 50-25-4 general powers as cited in the instrument [E1, locator requires confirmation]; OMB M-25-21 and M-25-22 high-impact AI classification, persuasive for states only [E2]; 2025 AI Agent Index, FAccT '26, on deployed autonomy in enterprise platforms [E1].

**Verification method.** Every entry in the enterprise register carries a review disposition and a trigger type. An auditor samples statewide-contract products with known AI features and confirms a corresponding register entry and disposition for each agency using them.

**Implementation dependencies.** None beyond the existing PSG revision process and the register established under R-13. No appropriation, statutory change, or procurement action required.

**Residual risk.** The activation trigger depends on the agency becoming aware, and vendors are not obliged to tell it. That gap closes only through the contract clause set at 6.6, and until then the trigger is weaker than the procurement trigger it supplements.

### 6.2 — SS-23-002, new §13: Notice, Explanation, and Human Re-determination (F-01, F-05, P1)

**Current text.** None. The duties exist only at GS-23-001 IV.1, IV.4 and IV.5 and on the public principles page, in a non-binding tier.

**Defect summary.** No binding instrument requires that a resident be told an automated system is involved in a matter affecting them, be given an explanation of an adverse outcome, or be given a route to a human re-determination; the source framework's human-alternative right was dropped in adaptation and nothing replaced it.

**Proposed text.**

> **13. Notice, Explanation, and Human Re-determination**
>
> For the purposes of this section, a **covered determination** is a decision, recommendation, score, ranking, or eligibility assessment produced or materially informed by an AI system that affects an individual's eligibility for, receipt of, reduction of, or termination of a public benefit, licence, permit, certification, employment, or access to an essential state service. Each agency shall designate covered determinations in the enterprise Artificial Intelligence register and shall record the designation decision for any AI system it declines to designate.
>
> Agencies shall:
>
> a. Provide notice, at or before the point of interaction, that an AI system is used in making or informing a covered determination. The notice shall be in plain language, shall identify the agency and the function the system performs, shall state that the individual may request human re-determination under paragraph (d), and shall conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA.
>
> b. On issuing an adverse covered determination, provide the individual with a written explanation stating the principal factors that led to the outcome, the role the AI system played, and the route to re-determination and appeal. The level of detail shall be calibrated to the consequence of the determination, and shall in every case be sufficient for the individual to identify what to contest.
>
> c. Not issue an adverse covered determination on the sole basis of an AI system output. A qualified employee designated under paragraph (e) shall review the output together with the underlying record and shall record their determination and its basis before the adverse determination issues.
>
> d. Provide, on request made within the period allowed for appeal, a human re-determination of the covered determination conducted by an employee who did not make the original determination and who has authority to reverse it. The agency shall complete the re-determination within ten business days of the request, or within any shorter period required by the programme's governing law, and shall notify the individual of the outcome in writing.
>
> e. Designate by name or position the employee or class of employees authorised to conduct review under paragraph (c) and re-determination under paragraph (d), record the designation in the register, and ensure each designee has completed the oversight-competence training required by §7.
>
> f. Provide a human alternative to any AI-mediated channel through which an individual may apply for, maintain, or appeal a benefit, licence, or service. The alternative shall be accessible, shall not impose additional cost or a longer processing time on the individual, and shall be described in the notice required by paragraph (a).
>
> g. Retain, for each covered determination, the notice issued, the explanation issued, the reviewing employee's recorded determination, any re-determination request and outcome, and the AI system version in use at the time. Retention shall follow the applicable schedule under the Georgia Records Act.
>
> Nothing in this section limits any right or procedure available to an individual under Chapter 13 of Title 50 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated or under the governing law of the programme concerned.

**Rationale.** This restores the element dropped from the source framework and does so in the tier that can carry it. The "covered determination" threshold is drawn deliberately narrower than "any AI use" so that the duty is achievable: it attaches where the consequence to the resident is highest and where due-process exposure is real, and it requires agencies to record the systems they decline to designate, which converts a scoping judgement into an auditable decision. Paragraph (c) also secures alignment with the requirement SB 444 imposes in the health-coverage context from 1 January 2027, enterprise-wide and in advance. Paragraph (f) is the human alternative; without it the section would create a right to complain about an automated channel while still requiring its use.

**Authority mapped.** Constitutional procedural due process where automated systems affect eligibility for, reduction of, or termination of benefits, licences or employment [E1]; O.C.G.A. § 50-13, Administrative Procedure Act, contested-case procedure [E1]; O.C.G.A. § 50-18-90 et seq., Georgia Records Act [E1]; ADA Title II, 28 C.F.R. § 35.200(b) and WCAG 2.1 Level AA [E1]; SB 444, adopted 5 May 2026, effective 1 January 2027 [E2]; GS-23-001 IV.1, IV.4, IV.5 and the public principles page, as the existing non-binding statements this provision operationalises [E1]; OSTP *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* (2022) Principle 5, as the source of the human-alternative concept — historical, not current federal authority [E2]; Colorado SB 26-189, persuasive and comparative only [E2].

**Verification method.** For any sampled covered determination, the agency can produce the notice, the explanation, the named reviewer's recorded determination, and the system version. An auditor or a court can test paragraph (c) directly by asking whether an adverse determination exists for which no reviewer record was made. Re-determination timeliness is measurable from request and outcome dates.

**Implementation dependencies.** Attorney General review of the interaction with existing contested-case procedure, since the re-determination route must not displace or duplicate a statutory appeal. Agency capacity for re-determination volume, which is unknown until covered determinations are designated in the register — this is the principal reason R-13 must precede R-2. No statutory change required; no new appropriation identified, though re-determination volume in a high-throughput programme may require reallocation.

**Residual risk.** A re-determination conducted by a reviewer who defers to the original output satisfies the text and defeats its purpose; that risk is addressed only partly by the automation-bias measures at 6.3 and R-22. The provision also does nothing for individuals who never learn a determination was adverse, and nothing for aggregate disparate impact, which is addressed at R-16.

### 6.3 — SS-23-002 §5: Meaningful Human Review (F-06, F-10, F-33, P1)

**Current text.** §5(c): "Establish a system of checks and balances involving multiple reviewers to minimize the risk of errors or inappropriate content." §5(d) requires business-owner review and approval before publication or decision use.

**Defect summary.** The family's principal human-oversight provision specifies none of the variables that determine whether review is a control or a formality, adds reviewers without countering automation bias, and applies its only binding form to generative content rather than to systems that make determinations.

**Proposed text.**

> Insert as §5(h), and amend §5(c) to cross-reference it:
>
> h. **Meaningful human review.** Where an AI system informs a covered determination as defined in §13, or where its output is relied on in an official capacity, the agency shall design review so that it operates as a control rather than a confirmation. The agency shall:
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;i. Designate the reviewing role by name or position and record it in the enterprise Artificial Intelligence register.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;ii. Ensure the reviewer has authority to reject, modify, or override the output without seeking further approval, and record that authority in the designation.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;iii. Ensure the reviewer is provided with the underlying record, the system output, the system's stated limitations, and any confidence or uncertainty information the system produces.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;iv. Specify the minimum time allowed for review, and monitor whether actual review time falls below it.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;v. Require the reviewer to reach and record an independent judgement on the underlying record before the AI system output is disclosed to them, wherever the determination is adverse and the process permits sequencing.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;vi. Sample not less than five per cent of reviewed determinations quarterly for blind re-review by a second reviewer, record the rate of disagreement between the original reviewer and the AI system output and between the two reviewers, and report both rates annually to the GTA Office of Artificial Intelligence.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;vii. Treat a reviewer-to-system disagreement rate below one per cent, sustained over two consecutive quarters, as an indicator that review may not be operating as a control, and investigate and record the cause.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;viii. Ensure each reviewer has completed the oversight-competence training required by §7.

**Rationale.** Every element here exists because its absence is how oversight provisions fail in practice. Naming the role and its override authority makes accountability locatable; supplying the underlying record and stated limitations makes independent judgement possible; sequencing independent judgement before output disclosure is the single most effective structural counter to automation bias; and the disagreement-rate indicator at (vii) is what converts an unfalsifiable duty into a measurable one. A reviewer who never disagrees with the system is not reviewing it, and the provision says so in a form an auditor can test. The five per cent quarterly sample is set low enough to be affordable in a high-throughput programme.

**Authority mapped.** SS-25-001 §1.1, human-in-the-loop review workflows for GenAI [E1]; GS-23-001 V.3 [E1]; SB 444, adopted, effective 1 January 2027, requiring a qualified clinical peer's participation before an AI-informed adverse coverage determination and barring the AI system from overriding that judgement [E2]; SS-23-002 §3(b)(i)(4), as the existing internal precedent for specific test discipline [E1]; automation-bias and verification-cost literature on the empirical performance of human review as a control [E2]; OMB M-25-21 minimum practices for high-impact AI, persuasive only [E2].

**Verification method.** The register holds the designation, the override authority, and the minimum review time for every system. Disagreement rates are reported annually and are directly falsifiable. An auditor tests (v) by examining the process sequence, and (vii) by examining whether a sustained low disagreement rate produced a recorded investigation.

**Implementation dependencies.** Process re-sequencing in any workflow that currently presents the AI output first, which is a configuration change in most case-management systems but may be a vendor dependency in some. Reviewer time is the real cost: (iv) and (vi) will make visible whether current staffing supports the review the state already claims to perform. Training curriculum under R-22. No statutory change.

**Residual risk.** Sequencing at (v) is qualified by "wherever the process permits," which a determined agency can read broadly. The disagreement-rate indicator can be gamed by manufacturing disagreements. Neither risk is eliminable by drafting; both are detectable in audit.

### 6.4 — SS-23-002, new §14: Authorization of Systems That Take Action (F-03, F-26, P1)

**Current text.** None in any binding instrument. GS-25-001's risk rubric states that AI should never have autonomous decision-making capability.

**Defect summary.** The binding tier governs what a model outputs for review and is silent on systems that act; the only statement on autonomy is hortatory, categorical, and contradicted by how enterprise AI is now deployed, leaving GTA with neither authority to permit a controlled deployment nor control over an uncontrolled one.

**Proposed text.**

> **14. Authorization of Systems That Take Action**
>
> An **acting AI system** is an AI system that, following an initial instruction, executes one or more actions affecting a state record, system, account, communication, payment, or third party without a human reviewing and approving each action before it occurs.
>
> No acting AI system shall operate in the state technology environment except under a written authorization issued by the GTA Office of Information Security. Agencies shall:
>
> a. Apply for authorization before enabling any acting AI system, including where the capability is activated within a product the agency already licenses.
>
> b. Identify in the application a single named employee as the **authorizing principal** for the system, who shall be accountable for every action the system takes and who shall hold a state role with authority sufficient to take those actions personally.
>
> c. State the **authority ceiling**: the specific actions the system is permitted to take, the systems and data it may access, any monetary limit per transaction and per day, and the categories of action it is prohibited from taking. Actions outside the ceiling shall be technically prevented, not merely prohibited.
>
> d. Issue the system credentials that are distinct from any human user's credentials, scoped to least privilege for the authorised actions, and time-limited to a period not exceeding the authorization term.
>
> e. Maintain a mechanism by which a named on-call employee can suspend the system's execution within fifteen minutes at any hour, and test that mechanism not less than quarterly, recording the result.
>
> f. Log every action taken, every tool or system invoked, every input that caused the action, and the authorizing principal, in a form sufficient to reconstruct the sequence of actions after the fact, retained under the applicable schedule.
>
> g. Where the system ingests content the state did not author, including retrieved web content, received electronic mail, uploaded documents, or third-party data, complete injection-resistance testing before authorization and on any change to the ingestion path, and record the results.
>
> h. Where the system delegates to, or receives delegation from, another AI system or a system operated by another party, identify each system in the delegation chain, its operator, and the authorizing principal accountable for the chain as a whole.
>
> i. Not enable persistent memory or multi-session task continuation without stating the retention period and the data categories retained in the authorization.
>
> Authorizations shall be issued for a term not exceeding twelve months, shall be recorded in the enterprise Artificial Intelligence register, and shall lapse on any change to the authority ceiling, the ingestion path, the delegation chain, or the underlying model version. GTA OIS may suspend an authorization at any time, and §10 applies to suspension as it applies to termination.

**Rationale.** The provision is deliberately permissive in form and restrictive in operation: it does not prohibit acting systems, because a prohibition GTA cannot enforce against capability arriving by vendor default is worse than an authorization regime it can. Every element maps to a risk category in the current joint international guidance — privilege at (c) and (d), design and configuration at (c) and (i), behavioural at (e) and (g), structural at (h), accountability at (b) and (f). The authorizing-principal construction at (b) is the load-bearing element: it ensures every action a system takes is attributable to a person who could lawfully have taken it, which is what makes the state's actions reviewable at all. Fifteen minutes at (e) is chosen because an action-taking system can complete a great deal of consequential activity in an hour and because a fifteen-minute on-call standard is already met by existing OIS incident practice.

**Authority mapped.** CISA, NSA, ASD's ACSC, Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, NCSC-NZ and NCSC-UK, *Careful Adoption of Agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI) Services*, 1 May 2026 — final joint guidance; persuasive for states, not binding [E1]; NIST COSAiS single-agent and multi-agent SP 800-53 overlays, in development, no announced publication date [E2]; NIST AI 100-2e2025 adversarial machine learning taxonomy [E1]; 2025 AI Agent Index, FAccT '26, on deployed autonomy levels in enterprise platforms and the concentration of documented incidents in prompt-injection vectors [E1]; SS-23-002 §10 and SS-08-045 [E1]; SS-25-001 §2.3, red-teaming and prompt testing, as the existing capability this provision draws on [E1].

**Verification method.** No acting system operates without a register entry holding an authorization, an authorizing principal, an authority ceiling, and a current interrupt test result. An auditor tests (c) by attempting an out-of-ceiling action and confirming technical prevention, and (e) by requiring an unannounced interrupt test. (f) is tested by asking an agency to reconstruct a specific past action sequence.

**Implementation dependencies.** Identity and credential tooling capable of issuing scoped non-human credentials, which most enterprise identity platforms in state use can do but which may require configuration work. Logging capacity, which has a storage cost. Injection-resistance testing capability, obtainable through the sandbox red-team function SS-25-001 §2.3 already establishes rather than through new hiring. The interrupt mechanism at (e) may require vendor cooperation where the agent is vendor-hosted, and where a vendor cannot provide it, authorization should be refused. On publication of the NIST agent overlays, this provision should be revised to reference them — which is why R-10's off-cycle trigger matters.

**Residual risk.** The definition turns on whether a human approves each action, and vendors will characterise event-triggered execution as pre-approved. The twelve-month term and the model-version lapse condition create administrative load that will generate pressure for blanket authorizations. And the provision governs the state's own systems; it does nothing about acting systems operated by third parties that interact with state systems from outside.

### 6.5 — SS-23-002 §5: Accessibility of AI-Mediated Interfaces and AI-Generated Content (F-04, P1)

**Current text.** None in SS-23-002. SS-25-001 §1.5 requires GenAI procurements to comply with GTA's IT security, privacy and accessibility standards.

**Defect summary.** No instrument addresses accessibility of AI-mediated interfaces or AI-generated content, and the applicable technical standard becomes mandatory for Georgia state agencies on 26 April 2027.

**Proposed text.**

> Insert as §5(i):
>
> i. **Accessibility.** Agencies shall ensure that every AI-mediated interface through which the public interacts with the agency, and all AI-generated content the agency publishes or provides to a member of the public, conforms to Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.1 Level AA. This applies to conversational interfaces, generated documents and portable document format files, machine-generated captions and transcripts, generated images provided with informational content, and any AI-mediated interface delivered under a contractual or licensing arrangement with a third party. Agencies shall:
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;i. Obtain and retain conformance evidence before deployment or publication, and record it in the enterprise Artificial Intelligence register.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;ii. Where AI generates content at volume, establish and record a conformance testing method covering a defined sample rather than each item, and state the sample rate.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;iii. Not rely on machine-generated captions, transcripts, or alternative text as the sole means of conformance for content on which an individual must rely to obtain or maintain a benefit, licence, or service, without human verification of accuracy.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;iv. Include conformance as an acceptance condition in every contract for an AI-mediated public interface, and provide for remediation at the vendor's cost.

**Rationale.** The obligation exists independently of the PSG family and the date is fixed; the purpose of the provision is to make the obligation locatable by the people who will otherwise miss it, and to attach it to the artifacts AI actually produces. Sub-item (iii) is the substantive addition: machine captioning and generated alternative text are the cheapest apparent route to conformance and the least reliable, and their failure mode in a benefits context is a notice failure. Sub-item (ii) makes the duty achievable at generation volume, which a per-item requirement would not be.

**Authority mapped.** ADA Title II, 28 C.F.R. pt. 35, and specifically the compliance dates at § 35.200(b) as amended by the interim final rule published 20 April 2026 at 91 Fed. Reg. 20902, moving the date for public entities serving populations of 50,000 or more to 26 April 2027; because population is calculated at state level, Georgia state agencies fall in that tier [E1; the rule remains an interim final rule and its final status requires confirmation, though the substantive WCAG 2.1 AA requirement is unchanged either way]; WCAG 2.1 Level AA as incorporated by reference [E1]; Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act [E1]; Title VI, as to accessibility of translated consequential communications [E1]; SS-25-001 §1.5 [E1].

**Verification method.** Conformance evidence exists in the register for every AI-mediated public interface and every published AI-generated content class. An auditor tests a live interface against WCAG 2.1 AA success criteria and tests (iii) by identifying whether any consequential communication relies on unverified machine captioning or alternative text.

**Implementation dependencies.** Accessibility testing capability, which agencies subject to existing state accessibility standards already hold in part. Vendor remediation terms under (iv) require the contract clause set at 6.6 and cannot be applied retroactively to existing agreements before renewal. The state's own instruments should be brought into conformance first, including republication of SS-25-001 §3.0's use-case guidance in accessible text form.

**Residual risk.** Conformance at deployment does not guarantee conformance of content generated later, which is why (ii) exists and why (ii) is the weakest element. Nothing here addresses the accessibility of an AI system's *reasoning* to a user who needs an explanation, which is the deeper problem and is addressed at 6.2(b).

### 6.6 — SS-23-002 §3(b): Vendor Requirements, Verification, and Named Standards (F-07, P1)

**Current text.** §3(b)(a)–(c) require adherence to GTA PSGs, adherence to "NIST guidance, risk management frameworks and standards for responsible artificial intelligence practices," and provision of copies of the contractor's and subcontractors' responsible AI framework and policies within thirty days of any update.

**Defect summary.** The NIST requirement names no publication, version, profile, or acceptance criterion and therefore cannot be breached determinately; the vendor duties are framed as attestation with no verification, no acceptance criteria, and no consequence for a deficient submission; and the §2 duties on non-agency parties identify no contractual or statutory hook.

**Proposed text.**

> Replace §3(b)(b) and insert §3(b)(d)–(f):
>
> b. Adhering to the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework 1.0 and, for generative systems, the NIST Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile (NIST AI 600-1), and to any successor or additional NIST publication that GTA designates in writing and publishes on the Office of Artificial Intelligence website. Designation shall state the publication, the version, and the date from which it applies to new and renewed agreements.
>
> d. **Verification distinguished from attestation.** For each requirement in this section, the solicitation shall state whether the state will accept the contractor's attestation or will require verification. Verification means evidence the state can test independently, including test reports with methodology, third-party audit or certification results, or state-conducted testing. The state shall require verification, and not attestation alone, for: bias and disparity testing results; pre-deployment evaluation results; accessibility conformance; and, for any system informing a covered determination under §13, evaluation conducted at the level of the deployed system rather than the underlying model alone.
>
> e. **Acceptance criteria and consequence.** The solicitation shall state the acceptance criteria for each required submission and shall provide that a submission failing to meet them is a material deficiency. A material deficiency is grounds for rejection of the response, and where discovered after award, for remediation at the contractor's cost within thirty days and for termination under §10 if unremedied.
>
> f. **Change notification.** The contractor shall notify the agency and the GTA Office of Artificial Intelligence not less than thirty days before, or where advance notice is not possible then within five business days after: a change to the underlying model or model version; the addition or removal of a subprocessor or third-party model or application programming interface dependency; the enabling of any capability by which the system takes action within the meaning of §14; or a change to the system's data retention or training practices in respect of state data. The contractor shall not use state data to train, fine-tune, or otherwise improve any model made available to another customer.

**Rationale.** Naming the publications converts an unenforceable reference into a testable one while the designation mechanism preserves the ability to keep pace without amending the standard — the pattern the family currently lacks. The verification-versus-attestation distinction at (d) is the substantive change: the available evidence establishes that most deployed AI systems disclose no internal safety results and no third-party testing, so a requirement to supply a framework collects documents and produces no assurance. Requiring system-level rather than model-level evaluation for consequential systems addresses the specific validity gap the evidence identifies. The change-notification duty at (f) addresses the failure mode that no other provision reaches: a live public-sector process whose behaviour changes because a vendor updated a model.

**Authority mapped.** NIST AI RMF 1.0 [E1]; NIST AI 600-1, Generative AI Profile, July 2024 [E1]; NIST SP 800-218A and NIST AI 100-2e2025 as candidate designations [E1]; NIST COSAiS overlay series, in development, as a future designation [E2]; 2025 AI Agent Index, FAccT '26 — of thirty prominent deployed agents, 25 disclose no internal safety results, 23 have no third-party testing information, and only 4 publish agent-specific system cards [E1]; ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as a certifiable procurement signal [E1]; ISO/IEC 42005:2025 as a designatable impact-assessment structure [E1]; Georgia state purchasing law and DOAS rules, as the hook the current provision lacks [E1]; Texas TRAIGA (HB 149), in force 1 January 2026, as a comparative model for tying consequence to substantiated framework compliance — persuasive only, not binding on Georgia [E1].

**Verification method.** The designation list exists and is dated. For any sampled AI contract, the file shows which requirements were verified rather than attested, the acceptance criteria applied, and the disposition. At least one recorded rejection or remediation demonstrates the consequence is real. Change notifications are held against register entries and can be reconciled with vendor release histories.

**Implementation dependencies.** DOAS process for statewide contract terms and legal review of the clause set. Vendor resistance to (f)'s advance-notice period and to the training-use prohibition should be expected, and the state's leverage is greatest at renewal — so the clause set reaches the existing estate only over a full renewal cycle, which is why R-6 is Immediate and its full effect is medium-term. State-conducted testing under (d) requires the evaluation capability addressed at R-23.

**Residual risk.** Verification is only as good as the state's capacity to test, and for foundation models beneath a vendor's product the state may be unable to verify anything at the model layer. The provision manages that by shifting the requirement to system-level evaluation, which the state can test, and accepts that model-layer assurance remains attestation-based.

### 6.7 — SS-23-002 §8: Definition of an AI Incident and Severity Taxonomy (F-09, P2, T4)

**Current text.** §8 requires reporting of "suspected misuse of AI systems, whether intentional or unintentional," taxonomised into five intentional and five unintentional categories. SS-25-001 §4.0 requires reporting of "significant incidents involving GenAI" to the GTA Office of AI within 48 hours.

**Defect summary.** A hard 48-hour reporting clock runs from an undefined trigger; no instrument defines an AI incident as distinct from misuse and from a data breach; there is no severity scale and no mapping to statutory notification duties.

**Proposed text.**

> Insert as §8(c), and amend SS-25-001 §4.0 to cross-reference it:
>
> c. **AI incidents.** An **AI incident** is an event in which an AI system behaves in a manner that departs from its intended or authorised operation, or produces an outcome that causes or could cause harm, whether or not any person misused the system. An AI incident is distinct from misuse under §8(a) and (b), and from a data breach, and a single event may constitute more than one. Agencies shall classify and report AI incidents as follows:
>
> | Level | Criteria | Report to | Within |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 — Critical | An AI system contributed to an adverse covered determination later found incorrect and affecting more than one individual; an acting AI system took an unauthorised action; state data was exposed; or a system's behaviour poses a risk to life or safety | Agency CIO, GTA OIS, and GTA Office of AI | 4 hours |
> | 2 — Serious | An AI system produced materially incorrect output relied on in an official capacity; a model or configuration change altered behaviour without authorization; an injection or manipulation attempt succeeded in part | Agency CIO and GTA Office of AI | 24 hours |
> | 3 — Moderate | Recurring output errors, drift detected against a monitoring threshold, or an accessibility or language-access failure in an AI-mediated interface | GTA Office of AI | 5 business days |
> | 4 — Minor | Isolated output error caught by review with no external effect | Agency internal record; included in the quarterly summary | Quarterly |
>
> An agency shall not delay a Level 1 or Level 2 report pending investigation. Where an AI incident involves personal information, the agency shall assess notification duties under Article 34 of Chapter 1 of Title 10 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated and under any applicable sectoral regime, and the AI incident clock in this section does not extend any statutory notification period. GTA OIS shall publish a quarterly enterprise summary of AI incidents by level, with agency and system identifiers withheld to the extent permitted, and §8's confidentiality provisions apply to reports made under this paragraph.

**Rationale.** The clock in SS-25-001 §4.0 is a genuine strength and it currently runs from a word no one has defined, which means it is either over- or under-triggered in every agency. Defining an incident by departure from authorised operation rather than by misuse captures the failure modes that matter most — drift, unauthorised model change, agent overreach, injection — none of which fits the existing ten-category misuse taxonomy. The four-hour Level 1 clock is set to match existing security incident practice rather than to create a new standard. Making the statutory notification period expressly non-extendable prevents the AI clock from being read as a safe harbour.

**Authority mapped.** SS-25-001 §4.0 [E1]; SS-23-002 §8 and O.C.G.A. § 50-18-72(a)(25), (a)(44) [E1]; O.C.G.A. § 10-1-910 et seq., Personal Identity Protection Act [E1]; PS-08-005 and existing enterprise security incident practice [E1]; NIST AI 600-1, MANAGE functions [E1]; the AI Incident Database and OECD AI Incidents Monitor as external corroboration sources for the taxonomy's categories [E2].

**Verification method.** The quarterly enterprise summary exists and shows incidents at more than one level. An auditor tests classification consistency across agencies for comparable events and tests whether any Level 1 or 2 event was reported outside its clock.

**Implementation dependencies.** None requiring appropriation. Detection capability is the real constraint: Level 3's drift criterion presupposes the monitoring thresholds established under R-15, so until those exist Level 3 will be under-reported and the summary should say so.

**Residual risk.** Self-classification will trend downward in the absence of audit. The taxonomy also depends on agencies recognising that a system has departed from authorised operation, which is precisely what a subtly drifting system makes difficult.

### 6.8 — All seven instruments: Review Cadence and Off-Cycle Revision Triggers (F-17, P2, T5)

**Current text.** PS-23-001 carries "Review Date: 01/15/2025." The other six instruments carry no review date. No instrument carries a version number or a revision trigger.

**Defect summary.** The family has no maintenance machinery: one lapsed review date across seven instruments, no versioning, no off-cycle revision trigger, and no named revision owner, against a legislative cadence that produced two AI statutes with 2027 commencement dates in a single biennium.

**Proposed text.**

> Insert into the metadata block of each instrument in the family, and record in PS-23-001 as an enterprise requirement:
>
> **Version:** [n.n] | **Review Date:** [twelve months from effective date] | **Revision Owner:** Director, GTA Office of Artificial Intelligence
>
> **Off-cycle revision.** The Revision Owner shall initiate review of this instrument, outside the scheduled cycle and within sixty days of the triggering event, on any of the following:
>
> a. Enactment of a Georgia statute imposing a duty on a state agency in respect of artificial intelligence or automated decision-making, or the commencement of such a statute already enacted;
>
> b. A final federal rule, or an amendment to the compliance date of a final federal rule, that applies to a state or local government entity and reaches artificial intelligence or automated systems;
>
> c. Publication by NIST of a final or public-draft publication that GTA has designated, or intends to designate, under SS-23-002 §3(b)(b); and
>
> d. A Level 1 AI incident under SS-23-002 §8(c), or an audit or independent evaluation finding that a provision of this family cannot be complied with or cannot be verified as written.
>
> The Revision Owner shall record, for each triggering event, whether revision was made and if not, why. Where an instrument is amended, the prior version shall be retained and published, and the related-instrument lists in every other instrument in the family shall be reconciled in the same action.

**Rationale.** This is the provision that makes the review cycle self-sustaining and the one whose absence explains most of the other findings in this run: the 2023 instruments did not become defective, they simply stopped moving while the environment moved. Trigger (a) would have caught SB 540 and SB 444; trigger (b) would have caught the ADA Title II date change; trigger (c) would have caught the NIST publication proliferation that made §3(b)(b) unenforceable. The requirement to record a decision not to revise converts the trigger from an aspiration into an auditable act. Reconciling related-instrument lists in the same action is what prevents the broken cross-reference in PS-23-001 from recurring.

**Authority mapped.** GTA's published PSG processes for creating and revising PSGs and for reviewing PSGs to determine continuing need [E1]; SB 540, effective 1 July 2027, and SB 444, effective 1 January 2027, as the events the current machinery did not register [E2]; 28 C.F.R. § 35.200(b) as amended, 91 Fed. Reg. 20902 [E1]; NIST COSAiS and the Cyber AI Profile as pending trigger (c) events [E2]; ISO/IEC 38507:2022 on governance-body obligations [E1].

**Verification method.** Every instrument carries a version, a review date, and a named owner. A revision log exists showing each triggering event and the disposition. An auditor tests by identifying a qualifying event and confirming a recorded decision within sixty days.

**Implementation dependencies.** None. This is achievable by administrative act using the existing PSG revision process, and it is the lowest-cost recommendation in this run relative to its effect.

**Residual risk.** The triggers are only as good as the surveillance that detects them, which is what the anchor register WATCH list at §7.2 exists to supply. A trigger recorded and declined is compliant, so the provision creates a record of inaction rather than preventing it — which is the correct trade, but should be understood as such.

### Register B — Assumptions and limitations

**What was assumed about GTA and agency practice.** That the four instruments supplied are the versions in force, which the live PSG site confirmed. That GTA's actual review practice is broader than PS-23-001's text — the AI Application and Tool Request Form and the AI programme pages suggest a general AI intake rather than a generative-only one — and that the F-02 defect is therefore textual rather than operational; the exposure is nonetheless real, because text is what an adversarial reader applies. That the AI Advisory Council and the Horizons Innovation Lab operate as described on ai.georgia.gov. That agencies file STARR reports and that the GenAI catalogue exists. That no covered agency is currently operating an authorised agentic system, since none could be identified — this assumption is fragile and R-3's inventory would test it. That "Agency" exclusions are a deliberate scope choice rather than a defect, per Part 1.3.

**What could not be assessed.** Whether any of the four unestablished roles is populated at any agency. Whether GTA has ever exercised its independent-evaluation right or completed an audit under SS-23-002 §12. The volume, turnaround, and disposition of Business Solutions Review requests, and therefore whether the review function is resourced to its load. The content of the SS-25-001 §3.0 use-case chart, which is published as a raster image, and therefore which use cases are actually prohibited. The content of the ethics framework page treated as a compliance referent by GS-25-002. Whether any state system would be a covered "operator" under SB 540, which is a legal determination for counsel on the enrolled text. Whether prompts and outputs are in fact being retained, and under what schedule. Training completion rates. Whether the SS-23-002 effective-date interval was deliberate.

**What would materially change the assessment.** Evidence that a covered agency is operating an agentic system with state credentials would raise F-03 from P1 to the top of the run and make R-3 urgent rather than immediate. Evidence that a predictive system informs a benefits, licensing, or employment determination at volume would raise F-01 and F-06 similarly and would make the absence of a re-determination route a live rather than a prospective exposure. Confirmation that GTA's review intake is in practice generative-only would convert F-02 from a textual defect into an operational one. A finalised ADA Title II rule with a different date, or its withdrawal, would change F-04's horizon but not its substance, since the underlying Title II obligation is independent of the technical standard's compliance date. Publication of the NIST agent overlays would supply a control baseline for §6.4 and would make R-17 substantially easier. Reintroduction of an HB 147 successor would convert R-28 from anticipatory positioning into compliance work with a statutory deadline. And an Attorney General opinion on whether the state is a covered operator under SB 540 would resolve the largest open legal question in this run.
